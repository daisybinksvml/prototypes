From field to glass

How a field becomes a lager.

Four stages. No shortcuts. Tap each one to see the work involved.

Barley doesn't grow itself. Somebody drills it, then spends the best part of a year negotiating with British weather — frost, drought and rain at exactly the wrong moment. Farming is mostly patience with mud on it.

When the crop finally comes good, it's cut, dried and safely stored — a whole year's work sat in a trailer. From here, the grain heads off to be prepared for brewing.

The barley is malted, then Hawkstone's brewers bring together water, malted barley, hops and yeast — and refuse to hurry any of it. No corners cut, and always lagered for longer. That's the whole trick.

Bold, yet balanced. A smooth body, top notes of light citrus, a subtle crisp bitterness and a malty backbone. Everything above happened so this bit could be effortless.

The people behind the pint

A lot of people worked hard for this pint.

Farmers grow the barley. Growers tend the hops. Maltsters prepare the grain. Brewers wait for it to become something worth drinking. You get the easy bit.

Up before it's light, drilling in autumn, feeding and protecting the crop through winter, then watching the sky all summer. One bad fortnight of weather can undo a year of work. The barley that survives is what a proper lager is built on.

Hops climb strings taller than a house and need training, tending and picking at exactly the right moment. British hop growing has been shrinking for generations — which is precisely why Hawkstone is putting money behind it. More on that below.

Takes the grain, the hops, the water and the yeast, and does everything properly — then leaves it alone. Lagering takes weeks, and the brewer's main skill is not being tempted to shortcut it. Hard to make. Easy to drink. That's the deal.

The BritHop revival

Putting British hops back on the map.

British hop farming has been shrinking since 1885. So Hawkstone has committed £2.7 million to using 100% British hops across its brews by 2028 — creating the demand that gives growers the confidence to plant more.

The Great BritHop Revival

British hops have been in steady decline since 1885 — for growers, the demand simply wasn't there to plant more. So Hawkstone invited over half of the British hop-growing industry to the brewery to talk about the problem.

The answer: a £2.7m commitment to use 100% British hops across all Hawkstone brews by 2028. That demand is expected to grow the entire British hop-farming acreage by around 15%.

It's a commitment, not a finished job — recent Hawkstone specials have already been brewed with 100% British hops, and the rest of the range follows by 2028.
